# Professional Positioning

## Purpose

This document captures the professional narrative that should guide the website, resume, project portfolio, LinkedIn profile, and startup applications.

This is not a keyword optimization exercise.

The goal is to present an accurate public profile that reflects the kind of engineer I have become.

## Core Narrative

I am an experienced software engineer who designs, simplifies, and finishes practical systems.

My background spans research, signal processing, backend engineering, cloud infrastructure, and software architecture.

The common thread is not a specific technology stack.

The common thread is system ownership: understanding a problem, defining boundaries, making tradeoffs, building the solution, and maintaining it over time.

## Differentiators

### Systems Builder

I naturally gravitate toward complete systems rather than isolated components.

Representative examples include:

- home automation platform
- infrastructure automation
- architecture documentation initiatives
- CODA/GODA parser-generator work
- personal web applications

The interesting part is the ability to reason across boundaries: software, infrastructure, data, hardware-adjacent concerns, deployment, operation, and documentation.

### Pragmatic Engineer

I prefer simple, understandable systems over fashionable complexity.

Recurring characteristics:

- skepticism of unnecessary abstraction
- skepticism of architecture without delivery
- preference for straightforward designs
- maintainability over novelty
- removal of complexity where possible

This should not be presented as anti-architecture.

It is better framed as architecture in service of delivery and long-term maintainability.

### Finisher

I tend to complete projects rather than only explore them.

Examples include:

- AWS certification
- GCP certification
- architecture documentation migration
- CODA/GODA implementation
- personal projects that were deployed and maintained

This is a meaningful differentiator because many engineers can prototype, but fewer consistently carry systems through to usable outcomes.

### Long-Term Thinker

I am comfortable with systems that evolve over years.

Examples include:

- home automation
- technical blog
- cloud and infrastructure skill development
- architecture initiatives

This favors incremental evolution over repeated rewrites.

### Broad Technical Background

My career is not a straight line.

It includes:

- electrical engineering
- signal processing
- medical imaging research
- agricultural signal processing and machine learning research
- backend engineering
- cloud infrastructure
- software architecture

This breadth needs to be framed deliberately.

Without framing, it can look unfocused.

With framing, it becomes evidence of range, adaptability, and systems thinking.

### Independent Learner

I routinely learn new technical domains independently.

Examples include:

- AWS
- GCP
- Kubernetes
- NixOS
- Terraform
- home automation hardware and software

Certifications are useful evidence, but they should not become the main story.

They support the broader narrative of disciplined learning and follow-through.

### Engineering For Real Users

Most of my work solves practical problems.

Examples include:

- home automation
- invoice management
- banking file formats
- architecture documentation
- operational tooling

The public profile should communicate that I am interested in useful systems, not technology for its own sake.

## Best Fit

This profile is likely to be valued by companies that need experienced engineers who can combine design and implementation.

Strong fits include:

- small engineering-led companies
- infrastructure and developer-tooling companies
- product companies with complex backend or platform concerns
- startups that value senior individual contributors
- teams that need architecture without architecture theater
- teams that value clear writing and technical ownership

The strongest fit is an environment where senior engineers are expected to build, document, reason about tradeoffs, and leave systems better than they found them.

## Poor Fit

This profile is less likely to be valued by companies that primarily reward:

- hype-driven technology adoption
- narrow specialization only
- high-visibility self-promotion
- process-heavy architecture detached from implementation
- pure product-growth engineering
- short-term feature throughput without concern for system quality

This does not mean those companies are bad.

It means they are less likely to recognize the parts of my profile that are most distinctive.

## Public Emphasis

The public profile should emphasize:

- architectural decisions
- engineering tradeoffs
- ownership from design through delivery
- systems that were shipped or maintained
- clear technical writing
- backend and infrastructure depth
- ability to operate across domains

It should avoid over-indexing on:

- technology lists
- certifications as identity
- job titles
- generic senior-engineer language
- vague claims of passion
- inflated personal branding

## Likely Underestimated Strengths

The following strengths are easy to understate because they do not always appear as simple resume bullets:

- translating ambiguous problems into maintainable systems
- finishing long-running technical work
- learning independently without requiring a formal project mandate
- communicating architecture clearly
- connecting research-style thinking with practical engineering
- working across software, infrastructure, and operational boundaries

These should be made visible through project writeups and concrete examples rather than abstract claims.

## Likely Concerns

A technically sophisticated reviewer may have concerns if the profile remains as-is.

Possible concerns:

- public profile may look less senior than the actual experience
- project ownership may not be obvious from job-title-oriented resume entries
- open-source visibility may be modest
- career breadth may look unfocused without a clear narrative
- certifications may look like credential collecting if not connected to real engineering work
- the existing website may read more like a personal blog than a professional engineering profile

The site and resume should answer these concerns directly through structure and evidence.

## CV Voice

The CV should sound like a practical engineer, not a corporate resume.

Preferred voice:

- direct, grounded, and technically honest
- engineer first, software specialist second
- personal enough to show developer and maker identity, but not casual or chatty
- confident without inflated senior-title language
- concrete about systems, ownership, and outcomes

Preferred phrases:

- developer and maker at heart
- practical drive to solve problems end to end
- analyze systems, take them apart, design them, and build them up again
- technical design, operational reliability, and domain understanding
- practical engineering across backend systems, cloud infrastructure, and system integration

Avoid:

- generic resume language
- exaggerated leadership claims
- vague claims of passion
- long technology keyword dumps
- internal project names without public context
- detailed partner onboarding timelines
- security scanner names or vulnerability details unless described generically
- exact infrastructure topology or access patterns
- commit-history-derived claims that cannot be explained without internal context
- local repository paths, personal email metadata, and generated-analysis notes
